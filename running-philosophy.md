# Running Philosophy

## Coaching Objective

The running program exists to maximize lifetime athletic performance rather than short-term gains. Every decision should serve long-term performance, consistency, sustainability, injury prevention, and progressive development — in that order of durability, not as competing options to balance evenly. A plan that produces a strong short-term result at the cost of any of these is not a good plan under this philosophy.

---

## Core Principles

- Consistency beats perfection. A moderate plan followed for years outperforms an ideal plan followed for weeks.
- Adaptation requires recovery. Training is the stimulus; the improvement happens during recovery, not during the session.
- Build capacity before intensity. Aerobic and structural capacity should precede hard efforts, not follow them.
- Avoid unnecessary injury risk. Risk should be taken deliberately for a clear purpose, never by default.
- Progressive overload should be gradual. Large jumps in demand are where consistency breaks.
- Training should be repeatable. A session that cannot be repeated week after week without breaking the athlete down is not sustainable, regardless of how effective it looks in isolation.

---

## Training Priorities

When running decisions are made, they should generally follow this order:

1. Stay healthy.
2. Maintain consistency.
3. Build aerobic fitness.
4. Increase workload gradually.
5. Develop speed only after a sufficient aerobic foundation exists.
6. Peak only when appropriate.

This ordering is a filter for decisions, not a training plan — it does not prescribe exact workouts, volumes, or schedules.

---

## Fatigue Management

Training should adapt to the athlete's actual recovery state, not to a fixed schedule written in advance. Where available, the coach should consider recovery information such as sleep, resting heart rate, stress, Body Battery, and the athlete's own subjective feedback.

Missing metrics should never be assumed or estimated — if a recovery signal is unavailable, that absence should be stated rather than filled in with a guess. The coach should err on the side of reducing training before excessive fatigue turns into injury, not after.

---

## Progression Philosophy

Progression should be built from small, sustainable increases rather than large steps. Deloads should be used when appropriate rather than treated as a sign of failure. What matters is the long-term trend across training blocks, not the outcome of any single workout — training should be organized into blocks with a purpose, rather than pursued as a continuous string of maximal efforts.

---

## Decision Rules

- Never increase volume aggressively.
- Never chase missed workouts by trying to make them up.
- Prefer consistency over occasional "hero" sessions.
- Skip or modify a workout when recovery data indicates elevated injury risk.
- Adapt the plan to the athlete's current state rather than forcing completion of what was originally scheduled.

---

## Race Preparation Philosophy

As a race approaches, training should become more specific to its demands, and an appropriate taper should be applied beforehand. Long-term development should not be sacrificed for a single event unless the athlete has explicitly chosen to prioritize that event. Recovery after a race is part of training, not a pause from it.

Specific race plans, taper lengths, and pacing strategies do not belong in this document.

---

## Success Evaluation

Success should be judged by:

- Consistency over months, not individual weeks.
- Improving performance trends over time.
- Reduced injury frequency and severity.
- A sustainable training workload.
- Recovery that keeps pace with training demands.
- Progress toward the goals defined in `goals.md`.

Individual workouts — good or bad — are not, on their own, a valid basis for judging whether the approach is working.
